  \begin{abstract}
  Experimental Tokamak fusion facilities like DIII-D frequently experience disruption events that result in a plasma current drop to zero. The existence of a computationally inexpensive model for identifying disruptive shots and accurately identifying time of disruption can assist the scientific community by auto-labeling shots for further analysis. We took a labeled plasma disruption dataset from the DIII-D Tokamak reactor, utilizing shot numbers 125500-186000 (nearly 40,000 total plasma shots) to develop a new disruption labeling model that can generate automated disruption labeling using only plasma current time series data. We train a CNN (Convolutional Neural Network) to perform boolean disruption/not-disruption prediction and achieve a 99\% recall and 91\% precision on our test dataset. We additionally develop a classical convolutional filter heuristic that is capable of accurately identifying time of disruption with a median of $0$ and standard deviation of $\qty{1.1}{\milli\s}$ on our test dataset.
  \end{abstract}
  \vspace{1.5em}
\end{strip}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A Tokamak is a torus-shaped magnetic confinement fusion reactor and is a leading candidate to produce controlled thermonuclear fusion power~\cite{WANG2023163}. A prominent challenge facing Tokamak reactors is the issue of disruption events caused by turbulent instability within the plasma. Such disruption events cause the internal reactor current ($I(t)$) to drop to zero and can potentially cause damage to the interior of the reactor. Abnormalities such as plasma disruptions are a prominent challenge for upcoming flagship fusion experiments such The International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER)~\cite{su16104089, WANG2023163}, which requires a $\qty{30}{\milli\s}$ warning time for successful disruption mitigation~\cite{deVries2016}.

Decades of Tokamak experiments have created a large corpus of fusion data, leading to a growing body of literature utilizing machine learning for problems infeasible to address with classical methods, including disruption prediction~\cite{Pavone_2023}. A variety of classical machine learning algorithms have demonstrated promise; support vector machines, fuzzy logic, generative topographic mapping, and artificial neural networks have all been studied on a broad range of Tokamaks across the United States, India, China, and Europe~\cite{Vega2022}. In the deep learning space, sequence based models such as Long-Short Term Memory Networks (LSTMs) show promise for dynamic time-series forecasting of plasma disruptions~\cite{Muruganandham03102025}.  A variety of approaches have also been proposed for knowledge transfer from existing devices to new ones, using hybrid architectures ~\cite{Zhu2020} and transfer learning~\cite{Zheng2023}. 


A large fraction of the data produced by long-running Tokamak fusion facilities such as the DIII-D reactor operated by General Atomics has already been labeled for training machine learning models. A team at Princeton trained a Convolutional Neural Network on Electron Cyclotron Emission imaging (ECEi) diagnostic data from the DIII-D tokamak, achieving an F1 score of $0.91$~\cite{Churchill2020}. Another team at MIT trained a neural network on 14 plasma signatures from $22669$ shots from the C-Mod, DIII-D, and EAST tokamaks, correctly identifying 84\% of disruptive events with a model AUC of $0.94$ and an early warning time of $\qty{15}{\milli\s}$. Similarly at Harvard, researchers achieved an AUC of $0.94$ on time-series disruption forecasting using a multilayer long/short-term memory network (LSTM) trained on $8959$ shots from DIII-D and JET, optimizing models for performance at deadlines of both $\qty{30}{\milli\s}$ and $\qty{1}{\s}$. Our CNN does not perform forecasting, but outperforms each of these results on classication with an exceptional AUC of $0.99$ (\ref{fig:roc}).

We test our time-of-disruption model on 889 disruptive shots in our test holdout set (Figure~\ref{fig:predictions_scatter}); note that plasma current drops to zero after a disruption, which is why we do not see many late predictions. Our predicted disruption time has a standard deviation of $\qty{1}{\milli\s}$, well within the $\qty{30}{\milli\s}$ ITER warning deadline (Figure~\ref{fig:heuristic_diff}); therefore, our model can be used to generate accurate disruption labels for every historical DIII-D plasma shot, giving researchers access to more training data than every before.

\section{Neural Net Classifier}
\label{sec:methods}

Our dataset consisted of $39438$ shot files from the DIII-D team, of which $13429$ are disruptive shots with a labeled disruption time $t_D$, and the rest are non-disruptive with a labeled disruption time $t_D=-1$. The labels appear to be generated through a combination of manual and automated methods, as evidenced by the presence of incorrect labels in a number of shots (Figure~\ref{fig:cnn_errors}).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{best_model/model_diagram.png}
  \caption{Model architecture for our disruption labeling Convolutional Neural Network. We have four convolutional layers and two fully connected layers, and utilize max pooling, batch normalization, and dropout, along with a ReLU activation function. The model input is normalized current sampled at $\qty{4}{\kilo\hertz}$ and the output is disruption probability for the shot. }
  \label{fig:model_diagram}
\end{figure}

Each shot in our dataset sampled reactor current at $\qty{4}{\kilo\hertz}$, giving us a discrete $1 \times m$ current vector $\mathbf{I}$ where the $m$th component is current at the $m$th timestep. 

\begin{equation}
  \label{current_tensor}
  \mathbf{I}_p=\begin{bmatrix} I_1 & \dots & I_m \end{bmatrix}^T
\end{equation}

We used an 80/10/10 train/test/dev split, which left us with 31542 shots in the training set. To prepare our data for the neural net classifier, we tested three different normalization methods, including per-shot min-max unit scale normalization,  per-shot Mean-Variance (meanvar) normalization, and dataset-wide meanvar normalization. We found most success with the dataset-wide meanvar approach. Letting the current mean and standard deviation across all shots be $\mu$ and $\sigma$, we computed a normalized shot tensor $I_\mathrm{N}$ for the arbitrary $i$th shot with $N_i$ timesteps. If the longest shot has $N$ timesteps, we pad the $i$th shot with $N-N_i$ trailing zeroes.

\begin{equation}
  \label{current_norm}
\mathbf{I}_{p,\mathrm{N}} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{1}{\sigma}(\mathbf{I}_p - \mu)\\[0.5em]
  \mathbf{0}_{(N-N_i) \times 1}
\end{bmatrix}  
\end{equation}





The normalized data was then fed into a Convolutional Neural Network (see Figure~\ref{fig:model_diagram}) consisting of four convolutional layers with stride $1$, each with batch normalization~\cite{bn}, a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function~\cite{relu}, and a final max pool layer~\cite{maxpool}. The convolution outputs were then fed into two fully connected layers with batch normalization, ReLU, and a dropout layer with a tunable dropout rate \verb|dropout| hyperparameter. The code was written in PyTorch~\cite{pytorch} and trained using the Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation) optimizer~\cite{adam} with weight decay and a learning rate tuned per-epoch with \verb|ReduceLROnPlateau| in "min" mode~\cite{lr}. We utilized the \verb|BCEWithLogitsLoss| loss function with configurable positive weight and gradient clipping, and stopped training when enough consecutive epochs showed no improvement.

We tuned our hyperparameters with Bayesian Optimization using the \verb|ExpectedImprovement| acquisition function with $\xi=0.05$~\cite{bayes}. We additionally inserted random samples every 5 trials to escape local minima. Our scoring strategy was a weighted $F_\beta$~\cite{fbeta}, where parameter $\beta$ is the weighted harmonic mean of precision and recall (Eq~\eqref{fbeta}). We chose $\beta=1.5$ to prioritize recall ($R$) over precision ($P$) and minimize false negatives, rejecting trials below a precision floor of 90\%. 


\begin{equation}
  \label{fbeta}
  F_\beta =  \frac{PR(1 + \beta^2)}{P\beta^2+R}
\end{equation}

We tuned model and architecture hyperparameters for 100+ trials on the Polaris supercomputer in Argonne National Lab, with each trial sampling fresh hyperparameters and training a new model for up to 200 epochs. Increasing the decision threshold gave us higher recall at the cost of precision. With a decision threshold of 50\%, our best model achieved a precision of 90.52\% and recall of 98.84\% on the test set, along with an F1 score of $0.9450$ and F2 score of $0.9706$. Due to our large dataset and relatively simple feature mapping, we see an unusally high AUC of $0.9902$ (Figure~\ref{fig:roc}). The tuned hyperparameters are presented in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}. 
          
The model we present surpasses each of these results with a much simpler input data stream.

The Gaussian is centered with $\mu\approx \qty{0}{\milli\s}$, $\sigma=\qty{1.1}{\milli\s}$ (\ref{sub:disruption_time_diff_root} ). }
  \label{fig:heuristic_diff}
\end{figure*}

For any given shot, let the current be $I(t)$, $t\in[0, T]$. We start by noting that shots with $\max{I(t)}\approx0$ are negative polarity shots and must be flipped before proceeding (Eq~\eqref{current_raw}). 


\begin{equation}
  I_\mathrm{raw}(t) = \begin{cases}
    -I(t) & \max(I)\approx0 \\
    I(t) & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  \label{current_raw}
\end{equation}

We then calculate a smoothed current $I_\mathrm{smooth}(t)$ by applying a moving average. We compute the moving average on our dataset as a discrete convolution with window size $K=T / S$, where $S=300$ is an experimentally determined smoothing parameter. Applying \verb|np.convolve| on the current tensor Eq~\eqref{current_tensor}, we get the $i$th component of the smooth current $\mathbf{I}_{p,\mathrm{smooth}}$ given by Eq~\ref{current_smooth}.

\begin{equation}
  \label{current_smooth}
  \mathbf{I}_{p,{\mathrm{smooth}_i}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=-K/2}^{K/2} I_{p,{i+k}}
\end{equation}

We then define the time of disruption heuristic filter $f(t)$ as the difference between the raw current in Eq~\eqref{current_raw} and smooth current in Eq~\eqref{current_smooth}. 



\begin{equation}
  \label{heuristic}
  f(t)=I_\mathrm{raw}(t) - I_\mathrm{smooth}(t)
\end{equation}

The global maximum of $f(t)$ is our predicted disruption start $t=t_0$. The first local minimum of $f(t)$ where  $t>t_0$ identifies the disruption end $t_f$.


\begin{align}
  \label{time_of_disrupt}
  t_0&=\max(f(t)) \\
  t_f&=\min(f(t)), \quad t>t_0
\end{align}


\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{best_model/heuristic_example.png}
  \caption{Two example shots (above) with their disruption heuristic $f(t)$ (below). On the left, we see shot \#125500 with a DIII-D label near the disruption start (Eq~\eqref{time_of_disrupt}); on the right, we see shot \#178323 with a DIII-D label near the disruption root (Eq~\eqref{time_of_disrupt_root}).
  }
  \label{fig:heuristic}
\end{figure}


Comparing $t_0$ to the labeled disruption times $\{t_D\}$ in our test set, we find that most of our predictions are early by $\Delta t = t_0-t_D \approx \qty{-4}{\milli\s}$ (Figure~\ref{sub:disruption_time_diff_start}). Therefore, we conclude that the DIII-D team labels shots mid-disruption, somewhere in the range $t_0\leq t \leq t_f$. If we use the first root $t_\mathrm{root}$ (Eq~\eqref{time_of_disrupt_root}) as the predicted disruption time, we see a normalized distribution of $\Delta t = t_\mathrm{root}-t_D$ centered around $\Delta t = 0$ in Figure~\ref{sub:disruption_time_diff_root}.

 \begin{equation}
  \label{time_of_disrupt_root}
  f(t_\mathrm{root})=0, \quad t_\mathrm{root}\in[t_0, t_f]
 \end{equation}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We train a multi-layer Convolutional Neural Network to classify shots from the DIII-D Tokamak Reactor as disruptive/non-disruptive using plasma current vs. time data. We use a $F_\beta=1.5$ performance metric to favor recall over precision and perform hyperparameter tuning over 100 trials; the trained classifier correctly identifies 99\% of disruptive shots on our test holdout set, producing less than 9\% false positives. We also develop a convolutional formula to identify the disruption time in disruptive shots with a median of $0$ and a standard deviation of $\qty{1}{\milli\s}$, with an option to additionally identify the full time-span of a disruption. Our algorithm can generate automated labeling of every DIII-D shot up to the current day, and should generalize well to other Tokamak reactors, providing a much larger labeled dataset to the scientific community for developing richer models.
